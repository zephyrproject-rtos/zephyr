Medical Device Regulation (MDR 2017/745)
########################################

.. warning::
   This document is for informational purposes only and does not constitute legal,
   regulatory, or conformity advice. Placing a medical device on the market and
   demonstrating conformity with the applicable requirements is the responsibility of the
   device manufacturer and depends on how Zephyr is configured and integrated. Consult the
   regulation itself and, where relevant, a notified body or a qualified regulatory expert.

Overview
********

**Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR)** governs the placing on the market and
putting into service of medical devices in the European Union. It applies since 26 May 2021,
replacing the earlier Medical Devices Directive (93/42/EEC), and its goal is to ensure a high
level of safety and performance for medical devices throughout their lifetime.

Many modern medical devices — from smart monitoring wearables to therapeutic and diagnostic
equipment — are built around embedded, connected, software-driven systems. For such devices
the MDR imposes requirements that touch software reliability, risk management, and, explicitly,
**information security**. These requirements are most relevant to firmware and platform choices.

This page explains how the MDR relates to the Zephyr Project. Zephyr is **not** a medical
device, is not placed on the market as one, and is not itself assessed or certified for MDR
conformity. Instead, Zephyr is a **component building block**: a manufacturer integrating
Zephyr into a medical device uses Zephyr's features and development process as part of the
technical evidence needed to meet the applicable requirements. Meeting the MDR remains the
manufacturer's responsibility.

Unlike paid standards, the regulation itself is freely available on EUR-Lex (see
`External resources`_). This page summarizes the parts most relevant to an embedded platform
and maps Zephyr's capabilities to them, rather than reproducing the regulatory text.

Structure and scope
*******************

The MDR is organized into articles and annexes. The parts most relevant to an embedded
software platform are the **General Safety and Performance Requirements (GSPR)** in Annex I,
in particular the requirements addressing electronic programmable systems and software.

Devices are assigned to risk **classes I, IIa, IIb and III** (increasing risk), which
determine the conformity assessment route and, for most classes, the involvement of a
**notified body**. The classification does not change the platform-level building blocks a
manufacturer needs, but it does affect the depth of evidence required.

.. list-table:: Annex I GSPR provisions most relevant to embedded software
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 20 80

   * - Provision
     - Scope
   * - 14.2(d)
     - Reducing, as far as possible, risks linked to the possible negative interaction
       between software and the IT environment in which it operates.
   * - 17.1
     - Devices that incorporate electronic programmable systems and software shall be
       designed to ensure repeatability, reliability and performance in line with their
       intended use, with appropriate measures for fault conditions.
   * - 17.2
     - Software shall be developed and manufactured in accordance with the state of the art,
       taking into account the development lifecycle, risk management (including
       **information security**), verification and validation.
   * - 17.4
     - Manufacturers shall set out minimum requirements concerning hardware, IT network
       characteristics and IT security measures, including **protection against unauthorised
       access**, necessary to run the software as intended.
   * - 18
     - Requirements for active devices and devices connected to them.

Supplementary guidance published by the Medical Device Coordination Group, notably
**MDCG 2019-16 on cybersecurity for medical devices**, elaborates how these requirements are
interpreted for connected and software-driven devices. The closely related
**Regulation (EU) 2017/746 (IVDR)** applies analogous requirements to in vitro diagnostic
devices.

Roles and responsibilities
**************************

The MDR assigns responsibilities across distinct economic operators. Understanding where
Zephyr sits clarifies which obligations apply to whom:

**Manufacturer**
  Designs, produces and places the device on the market under its own name, and bears
  responsibility for conformity, technical documentation, and post-market surveillance.

**Importer / distributor**
  Makes the device available on the EU market; verifies that the manufacturer met its
  obligations.

**Notified body**
  Independent conformity assessment body that audits and certifies devices in the applicable
  risk classes.

A developer building a medical device on top of Zephyr acts as (part of) the
**manufacturer**, and Zephyr is a software component within that device. Zephyr provides
building blocks that make the applicable requirements achievable; it does not discharge the
manufacturer's obligations.

How Zephyr supports the MDR
***************************

The table below maps the software- and security-relevant GSPR themes to Zephyr features and
documentation that a manufacturer can use as building blocks. This is an indicative mapping,
not a conformity claim: whether a specific requirement is met depends on the device's
configuration, hardware, intended use, and integration.

.. list-table:: MDR software/security themes mapped to Zephyr building blocks
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 30 70

   * - MDR theme
     - Relevant Zephyr building blocks
   * - Repeatability, reliability and performance (17.1)
     - Deterministic real-time scheduling, resource and memory management, watchdog support,
       and defined fault handling contributing to reliable operation.
   * - Software lifecycle and risk management (17.2)
     - A documented security process and threat model (:ref:`security-overview`),
       :ref:`secure coding guidelines <coding_guidelines>`, a coordinated vulnerability
       handling process (:ref:`reporting`), and SBOM generation via :ref:`west spdx
       <west-spdx>` for supply-chain transparency.
   * - Information security in the lifecycle (17.2)
     - Cryptographic services (PSA Crypto API, TLS/DTLS) and verified/secure boot with image
       signing (MCUboot, :ref:`west sign <west-sign>`). See :ref:`security-overview`.
   * - Protection against unauthorised access (17.4)
     - Thread-level privilege separation via :ref:`user mode <memory_domain>`, hardware
       memory protection, :ref:`system call verification <syscall_verification>`, and the
       :ref:`hardening tool <hardening>` to reduce attack surface.
   * - Secure and reliable updates (17.2, 17.4)
     - Integrity-checked firmware updates (:ref:`dfu`) built on signed images, enabling
       vulnerabilities to be remediated over the device lifetime.
   * - Minimizing attack surface (17.4)
     - Configurable networking stack and the ability to disable unused interfaces and
       services via :ref:`Kconfig <kconfig>` and the :ref:`hardening tool <hardening>`.

Software lifecycle and state of the art
========================================

Article 17.2 requires software to be developed "in accordance with the state of the art".
For medical device software this state of the art is expressed primarily through
**IEC 62304** (medical device software lifecycle processes) and **IEC 81001-5-1** (health
software security activities in the product lifecycle). Zephyr does not implement these
processes on a manufacturer's behalf, but supports them through:

* A documented security process and threat model: :ref:`security-overview`.
* :ref:`Secure coding guidelines <coding_guidelines>` and :ref:`secure development
  practices <secure code>`.
* A coordinated vulnerability handling and disclosure process (PSIRT): :ref:`reporting`.
* Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) generation for supply-chain transparency via
  :ref:`west spdx <west-spdx>`.

Conformity and shared responsibility
************************************

Zephyr is not assessed or certified for MDR conformity, and using Zephyr does not by itself
make a device compliant. Conformity is demonstrated per device by the manufacturer, according
to the device's risk class and intended use, and — for most classes — with the involvement of
a notified body. Zephyr's role is to provide the technical building blocks and process
evidence that make reaching the applicable requirements practical, under a shared-responsibility
model where the final configuration and integration decisions rest with the manufacturer.
